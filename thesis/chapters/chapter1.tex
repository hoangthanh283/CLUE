\section{Background and Context}
\label{sec:intro-background}

Modern organisations process a continuous stream of \emph{visually-rich
documents} (forms, invoices, receipts, contracts, and reports) whose meaning
depends not only on the words they contain but on \emph{where} those words sit on
the page. Extracting structured information from such documents is the task of
\gls{kie}, the cornerstone of document-understanding systems deployed in finance,
logistics, healthcare, and public administration. The dominant approach casts
\gls{kie} as token-level sequence labelling over a tri-modal input: text obtained
by \gls{ocr}, the two-dimensional layout encoded as bounding boxes, and the page
image. Multimodal document encoders such as LayoutLMv3~\citep{huang2022layoutlmv3},
LiLT~\citep{wang2022lilt}, and BROS~\citep{hong2022bros} fuse these signals and
define the state of the art.

Deployed document systems are not static. New document types appear, annotation
schemas are extended, and the distribution of incoming documents drifts as an
organisation enters new markets or adopts new templates. A model must therefore
\emph{evolve}, learning new entity types and new domains over time, without
re-training from scratch on all historical data, which is often impossible because
earlier data is unavailable, expensive to store, or restricted for privacy reasons
(\gls{pii} under \gls{gdpr}). This is the setting of \gls{cl}: learning a sequence
of tasks while retaining performance on those seen earlier.

The central obstacle to \gls{cl} is \gls{cf}~\citep{mccloskey1989catastrophic}:
when a neural network is fine-tuned on a new task, gradient updates overwrite the
parameters that encoded earlier tasks, and performance on those tasks collapses. A
large literature has developed remedies: regularising important
weights~\citep{kirkpatrick2017ewc}, distilling from past models~\citep{li2017lwf},
replaying stored exemplars~\citep{rolnick2019er,buzzega2020der}, learning
task-specific prompts~\citep{wang2022l2p,wang2022dualprompt,smith2023coda}, or
allocating low-rank adapters per task~\citep{wang2023olora}. Almost all of these
methods, however, were developed and evaluated on \emph{unimodal} image- or
text-classification benchmarks, and they treat the network as a homogeneous block:
regularisation is applied uniformly across parameters, or layers are frozen by
hand-tuned heuristics.

For a \emph{tri-modal} document encoder this uniform treatment is unsatisfying,
because the network is manifestly heterogeneous: it contains a text stream, a
visual/patch stream, layout (2D position) embeddings, shared self-attention and
feed-forward blocks at increasing depth, and a task head. These parts are trained
on different signals, carry different inductive biases, and, there is every reason
to suspect, are unlikely to age at the same rate under a shifting task
distribution. Yet the prevailing
remedies are blind to this structure: elastic-weight
penalties~\citep{kirkpatrick2017ewc} apply one importance-weighted quadratic term
across all parameters, distillation~\citep{li2017lwf} matches whole-network
outputs, and heuristic layer freezing draws an arbitrary depth-wise cut that
ignores the modality a layer serves. Even the modern parameter-efficient and
modular families (low-rank adapters~\citep{wang2023olora} and prompt
pools~\citep{wang2022l2p,wang2022dualprompt,smith2023coda}) only sharpen the
question rather than answer it: deciding \emph{which} weights to adapt or
\emph{where} to inject a prompt implicitly presupposes that one already knows which
part of the encoder is at risk. Evidence from unimodal vision transformers shows
that forgetting is far from uniform across depth and is concentrated in identifiable
layers~\citep{ramasesh2021anatomy}, and recent probes of multimodal models report
modality-dependent degradation~\citep{zhai2023investigating}; but for a document
encoder that jointly reasons over text, layout, and pixels, \emph{no prior work has
measured where: at which depth, and in which input stream or the head, forgetting
concentrates}, nor whether that pattern differs from a unimodal encoder. That
measurement is the missing prerequisite this thesis supplies.

Answering that question (which component forgets most, and whether the pattern is
stable across backbones and continual scenarios) is the prerequisite for designing a
\gls{cl} method that spends its capacity where forgetting actually happens rather than
spreading it thinly across the whole network. This thesis therefore takes a
\emph{measure-first, then-fix} stance: it first \emph{diagnoses} where forgetting lives in a
multimodal document encoder, then uses the diagnosis to rule remedies in or out, and only
then proposes a remedy whose design follows from both.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro-problem}

\paragraph{Input.} A stream of document-understanding tasks
$\mathcal{T}_1,\dots,\mathcal{T}_T$ arriving one after another. Each task is a set of scanned
documents, each given as a page image, its \gls{ocr} tokens, and their two-dimensional
bounding boxes, annotated with token-level \gls{bio} entity labels for \gls{kie}. The stream
follows one of three regimes: \gls{cil}, where the label space grows from session to session
(FUNSD, CORD, WildReceipt sessions); \gls{dil}, where the label space is fixed and the document
type shifts (forms to receipts, or one XFUND language to the next); and a mixed regime. The
learner starts from a pre-trained tri-modal encoder (LayoutLMv3; LiLT, BROS, and a text-only
BERT as secondary backbones) with a linear token-classification head. Past raw documents are
not retained, for privacy and storage reasons; at most a bounded memory may be kept.

\paragraph{Output.} A single model that, after task $\mathcal{T}_T$, extracts entities from
documents of \emph{every} task seen so far. It is measured by average entity-F1 (AA), backward
transfer (BWT), forgetting, and the size of its memory in bytes.

\paragraph{Research questions.} The thesis asks two questions, in this order:
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (diagnosis).] Where in a heterogeneous multimodal encoder (the text, layout, and
        visual streams, the shared layers by depth, or the classifier head) does catastrophic
        forgetting concentrate under sequential \gls{kie}, and is that locus specific to
        multimodal fusion or general to the architecture?
  \item[RQ2 (remedy).] Given the locus, what must a memory-bounded remedy supply there to
        recover joint-training accuracy, and \emph{which} stored evidence should it re-expose
        for a given incoming document?
\end{description}

\paragraph{Motivation and challenges.} Five difficulties make these questions both necessary
and hard:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Deployment pressure.} Deployed systems must absorb new schemas and domains, and
        re-training from scratch on all historical data is usually infeasible.
  \item \emph{Structure-blind methods.} Existing \gls{cl} methods regularise uniformly or freeze
        layers heuristically, and parameter-efficient or modular methods presuppose knowing
        where to intervene; none tells us where that is for a document encoder.
  \item \emph{Heterogeneous encoders.} A tri-modal encoder has several input streams, shared
        layers, and a head; one modality could carry all of the forgetting, or none of it.
  \item \emph{Dense token labelling.} \gls{kie} labels every token of a long document, with a
        dominant background class and rare entity classes; most \gls{cl} methods were built for
        image or sentence classification and do not transfer directly.
  \item \emph{Memory that cannot be summarised cheaply.} Document features are close to full
        rank (a rank-16 projection keeps only $43\%$ of the variance of the head-input
        features), so a small, faithful summary of past tasks does not exist, while raw replay
        is costly in storage and in privacy.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:intro-objectives}

The thesis pursues four objectives, one per step of the approach:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Diagnose where forgetting lives (RQ1).} Build a per-component diagnostic
        protocol: controlled masking of the input modalities of a single LayoutLMv3 (holding
        the architecture fixed), read out through linear \gls{cka} drift, per-group Fisher
        information, and old-task-Fisher-weighted parameter displacement at every task
        boundary, and repeated on LiLT, BROS, and a text-only BERT
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, \S\ref{sec:method-diagnostic}).
  \item \textbf{Falsify the remedies the diagnosis rules out (RQ2).} Test regularisation,
        distillation, prompt and LoRA methods, eval-time and marginal-level corrections, and
        summarised replay against the diagnosed locus, and identify the single reason they fail
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, \S\S\ref{sec:results-remedy}--\ref{sec:results-falsification-ext}).
  \item \textbf{Replay at the locus, within a bounded memory (RQ2).} Establish what replay must
        preserve to work, and show that a compressed latent memory respecting it (CoLaR,
        per-document SVD factors at layer $k$) keeps replay-level accuracy at a fraction of the
        storage (\S\ref{sec:results-falsification-ext}).
  \item \textbf{Route the replay lexically (RQ2).} Use LexSlot's drift-immune
        \gls{ocr}-vocabulary signature, designed as the gate of a slot memory at the diagnosed
        locus, to decide which stored document latents are replayed for the incoming document
        (\S\ref{sec:method-lexslot}).
\end{enumerate}
\noindent All results are reported over three seeds on a released benchmark (FUNSD, CORD,
SROIE, extended with WildReceipt and XFUND) under one configuration system
(Chapters~\ref{ch:implementation} and~\ref{ch:experimental-setup}).

\section{Achievements}
\label{sec:intro-achievements}

\noindent\emph{Contributions at a glance.} The thesis makes four contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a \textbf{forgetting-localisation diagnosis} of tri-modal document encoders, showing
        that forgetting is output-side, architecture-general, and conserved under constraint;
  \item a \textbf{falsification of the buffer-free remedies} with one identified cause, the
        readout-marginal snap on a drifting representation, and the law that replay must
        preserve \emph{whole-document consistency};
  \item \textbf{lexically-routed latent replay}, a remedy derived from both: a compressed,
        per-document latent memory (CoLaR) at the diagnosed locus, with LexSlot's lexical
        signature as the gate that selects what is replayed;
  \item the \textbf{FCS-CL benchmark} and reproducible diagnostic tooling.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The remainder of this section expands each contribution.

\noindent\textbf{Where forgetting lives (RQ1).} We introduce, to our knowledge, the first
protocol that localises \gls{cf} in a multimodal document encoder along network \emph{depth}
and across its \emph{separable} parts: the three input streams (text, layout, visual), the
shared self-attention and feed-forward blocks at each depth, and the classifier head. We are
deliberate about what is separable: LayoutLMv3 is a \emph{single-stream} encoder, so after
the input embeddings text and visual tokens share the same attention projections and no
``fusion'' \emph{parameter} group is identifiable; we therefore measure the modalities'
contributions \emph{behaviourally}, by masking the input streams of a single LayoutLMv3, and
localise \emph{parameter} forgetting by depth, stream, and head. Three findings follow.
Forgetting is \emph{output-side}: under naive sequential fine-tuning an $88$-F1 task
collapses to $\sim$2 F1 after one further task, the head carries more than an order of
magnitude more Fisher importance than any other group, and per-token CKA drift rises
monotonically with depth (from $1.00$ at the input encoders to $0.25$ at the late layers and
$0.16$ at the head), while the input encoders barely move. It is \emph{architecture-general}:
the same head-concentrated pattern holds on LiLT, BROS, and a text-only BERT, so the locus is
the general output side, not a multimodal artefact, and we do \emph{not} claim a
fusion-specific effect. And it is \emph{conserved}: freezing everything after the first task
forgets nothing but learns nothing (AA $41.1$), while freezing only the diagnosed head and
late layers leaves naive-level forgetting (BWT $-76.8$) and the early and mid layers, stable
under naive training, now drift (CKA $\approx 1.0 \rightarrow 0.19$). The drift is moved, not
removed; a fixed consolidation budget recovers $\sim$85 AA when placed on the head/late locus
but collapses to $42.6$ AA when spread uniformly across depth.

\noindent\textbf{Why buffer-free remedies fail (RQ2).} The benchmark ordering follows the
diagnosis: replay\,$\gg$\,regularisation\,$\gg$\,distillation. Replay, which re-exposes
old-class targets at the head, reaches the joint upper bound on domain-incremental learning
(AA $\approx 88$ vs the oracle's $88.7$, backward transfer $\approx 0$); EWC curbs forgetting
under a domain shift; LwF and the prompt/LoRA families track the naive bound. A per-boundary
root-cause analysis names the single mechanism: the head's output marginal on old tasks snaps
to the just-trained task's label marginal (cosine $0.997$), and classes that are not
re-exercised are extinguished to $0.0$ F1 under every modality mask. Pre-registered eval-time
and marginal-level corrections all recover nothing, because the old-class probability mass has
already been destroyed. What replay must preserve is then isolated by a falsification ladder at
a fixed layer: replaying real whole documents retains AA $87.3$, while a rank-16 summary
($41.9$), per-class Gaussians ($39.4$), and even real $k$-means centroids ($36.7$) collapse.
The binding of feature, position, and label within one document is the necessary ingredient.

\noindent\textbf{Lexically-routed latent replay (RQ2).} Individual documents, unlike the pooled
feature space, \emph{are} low-rank. CoLaR stores each replayed document as rank-$r$ SVD factors
of its layer-$k$ activations, which keeps whole-document binding and reaches AA $87.6$ with a
$60$\,MB memory, $2.7\times$ smaller than raw replay and within $1.4$ points of the joint
oracle, with no raw document retained. LexSlot, an isolated slot memory at the head and late
layers gated by a drift-immune \gls{ocr}-vocabulary signature, reaches AA $87.3 \pm 1.0$ with
backward transfer $-2.3 \pm 1.2$ with a compact 200-exemplar buffer, forgetting less than plain
replay ($-2.8$/$-3.4$). The thesis joins the two: LexSlot's signature becomes the \emph{gate}
and CoLaR the \emph{memory}, so that for each incoming document the replay draws the stored
latents whose vocabulary is nearest (or farthest, or from the nearest task), rather than
sampling the store uniformly. The diagnosis decides \emph{where} the memory attaches; the
lexical signature, immune to the representation drift that defeats every buffer-free method,
decides \emph{what} is replayed.

\noindent\textbf{The FCS-CL benchmark and reproducible tooling.} We provide a unified \gls{cl}
benchmark over five document datasets (FUNSD, CORD, SROIE, WildReceipt, XFUND) with
class-incremental, domain-incremental, mixed, large-scale, and cross-lingual scenarios, a
baseline suite spanning the major method families, and per-component instrumentation, all
under one configuration system and a tracked experiment log, enabling the like-for-like
comparison that the fragmented prior literature has lacked.

\section{Overview of the Dissertation}
\label{sec:intro-overview}

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:background}} reviews document understanding and \gls{ie},
the \gls{cl} problem and its method families, the nascent literature on \gls{cl}
for \gls{ie}, evidence on where forgetting lives in multimodal models, and the
backbones relevant to this work; it closes by identifying the research gaps the
thesis addresses.
\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}} presents the methodological
contributions: the per-component forgetting diagnostic (RQ1), the remedy design space it
opens, LexSlot's lexically-gated slot memory, and the lexically-routed latent replay that
joins that gate to a compressed per-document memory (RQ2).
\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}} describes the framework, the
document-\gls{ie} pipeline, and the system architecture, including the
instrumentation hooks and experiment tracking.
\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:experimental-setup}} details the datasets, the continual
task construction, the model and training configuration, the baselines, and the
evaluation methodology.
\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:results}} answers RQ1 with the diagnosis and its stability across
seeds, task orders, and backbones, then answers RQ2: the benchmark ordering, the root cause of
the buffer-free failures, the whole-document consistency law, CoLaR, and lexically-routed
replay, with ablations, a computational-cost analysis, and threats to validity.
\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}} states limitations, conclusions, and future
work.
